# Product

## Register

product

## Users

People beginning to learn Morse code, returning hobbyists rebuilding recognition speed, and radio-curious learners who want short, focused practice sessions on phones, tablets, or desktop computers. Their primary job is to connect a heard or visible signal to a letter, then build recall through repetition without setup friction.

## Product Purpose

Morse Trainer teaches a focused starter alphabet through three complementary modes: guided lessons, self-paced drills, and a timed sprint. The same learning flow can be experienced through three distinct vintage-machine directions—an amber terminal, a broadcast teletext page, and a 1983 pocket trainer—without changing the underlying rules or progress. Success means the learner can start immediately, hear every signal clearly, understand feedback at a glance, and steadily improve accuracy and recall speed.

## Brand Personality

Focused, tactile, nostalgic. The product should feel like a real-purpose instrument with the small charm of vintage electronics: confident and playful, never childish or theatrical.

## Anti-references

- Generic SaaS dashboards with interchangeable glass cards, gradients, and decorative metrics.
- Childlike gamification that turns practice into a noisy reward loop.
- Retro styling that sacrifices readable type, accessible contrast, or familiar controls for visual novelty.
- A static concept gallery: every direction must remain a fully usable version of the same learning tool.

## Design Principles

- Make the signal the strongest element on every screen.
- Preserve one predictable learning model across all three visual directions.
- Let each machine metaphor shape the controls, typography, and feedback without obscuring function.
- Reward progress with immediate, calm feedback instead of interruption or spectacle.
- Adapt structurally to the device: focused handheld controls on mobile and useful learning context on larger screens.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Target WCAG 2.2 AA. Support keyboard and touch input, visible focus, 44px minimum touch targets, reduced motion, zoom and large text without horizontal scrolling, and status feedback that does not rely on color alone. Morse signals must remain visible as patterns alongside audio so deaf, hard-of-hearing, muted-device, and sound-sensitive learners can use the core experience.
